# 02 · Notes & Ownership

**One sentence:** Money is notes — private hash commitments owned by post-quantum keys — and addresses are published bundles of the keys needed to pay someone, never touching a blockchain.

**Requires:** [01-CRYPTO](01-CRYPTO.md)

## Notes

A **note** commits to `(asset, value, owner_pk, spend anchor, randomness)` under a domain-separated **Poseidon2** sponge (`kernel2/src/note.rs`). Notes exist only in their owner's client-side data; no chain, relay, or third party ever sees one. `randomness` makes commitments unlinkable; `owner_pk` is a **one-time WOTS+ public key** — one per note, which is what the section below is about — or a [hash-lock condition](06-PAYMENTS.md), making conditional notes ordinary notes. The anchor is inside the commitment so a spend cannot pair the note with a foreign key and mint a fresh nullifier for it — see the address section below for what the anchor is and why it is not the key itself.

**v1 contract scope (locked):** fungible assets — issue, transfer, burn. The kernel interface is written for generality; arbitrary Rust contracts are v2 and change no consensus.

## Spending

A spend is authorized by a **WOTS+ signature** over the transfer's bundle hash, verified in-circuit ([04](04-PROOFS.md)). v1 shape is **one input, exactly two outputs** (payment + change; a single-recipient payment carries a genuine zero-amount change note, so every hop looks alike). Value is conserved exactly — limb-wise with carries over range-checked 16-bit limbs in the circuit, and checked `u64` arithmetic on the host, where an overflowing sum is invalid and never wrapped (`kernel2/src/transfer.rs`, `kernel2/src/amount.rs`). What makes a spend *final* is the record layer ([03](03-RECORDS.md)); what makes it *valid* is the proof layer ([04](04-PROOFS.md)).

## Addresses, and a design error this spec carried

**The previous version of this section did not work, and it is worth stating why
before stating the fix.** It said: an address is `(spend_root, scan_kem_pk)`; the
sender encapsulates to the scan key and *derives the note's one-time owner key*.
That construction is self-refuting. If the owner key is a function of the shared
secret, then everyone who can decapsulate can derive it — including the delegated
scan-key watcher the same paragraph called "untrusted for funds", and including
**the sender**, who chose the shared secret. The sender could spend the note it
had just paid out.

The deeper reason is not a slip of wording. With elliptic curves you can tweak a
public key publicly and its secret privately, which is exactly what stealth
addresses need. A hash-based one-time public key is a hash tree over its own
secret; there is no public operation that produces one whose preimage only
someone else knows. Getting that from hashes alone would be key agreement from a
one-way function, which is not available. **The property has to come from
somewhere other than the key derivation.**

### The fix: the note commits to a spend *anchor*, not to a key

A note commits to `t = H(nullifier_key)` — the **anchor** — instead of to the
nullifier key itself. Spending requires showing, in-circuit, a preimage of the
committed anchor (`air/src/transfer_air.rs`, constraint 32). Everything a payer
needs to build a note is then public: the asset, the amount, the one-time WOTS+
public key, the anchor, and the blinding. Nothing a payer holds lets it spend.

**This also closes a live privacy leak.** Before the anchor, the note committed
to the nullifier key, so the payer necessarily learned it — and the nullifier is
a deterministic function of that key and the commitment. Every payer kept a
permanent watch on the exact moment its payment was spent onward. With the
anchor, the payer holds `t` and can compute nothing.

Cost: one extra sponge row and nine columns. The section had two spare rows
under the power-of-two trace height, so the anchor was close to free — see
[04](04-PROOFS.md) for the measurement.

### What an address is now, and what is still interactive

An address carries the ML-KEM-768(+X25519) scan key, the SLH-DSA identity chain,
and **slots**: pre-issued `(index, owner_pk, anchor, randomness)` tuples the payee
generated from its own seed. A payer takes the next unused slot, builds the note,
and encrypts the envelope to the scan key. The payee reconstructs everything from
`(seed, index)`. The one-time signing key is generated by the payee and never
leaves it, so the sender-can-spend problem does not arise at all.

Slots must be handed over once, on first contact, and replenished. **That is a
real round trip, and this spec previously claimed there was none.** In practice
it is already paid for: [05](05-NETWORK.md) states that two parties exchange
address records in-band on first contact and that you cannot pay a stranger you
have never messaged. The honest claim is *no invoice per payment*, not *no
interaction ever*.

A reusable anchor would remove even that, at a price: one long-lived secret would
enter every spend's witness, so the zero-knowledge property of the proof would
become load-bearing for more than privacy. The circuit does not care which
lifetime an anchor has — that choice lives at the address layer and can change
without touching consensus. It is left open deliberately ([99](99-OPEN-PROBLEMS.md)).

## One-time keys: never re-sign, and never *forget what you signed*

Moving spend authorization from SLH-DSA to WOTS+ ([04](04-PROOFS.md)) buys most of the proving cost back and introduces one new failure class, which is severe and must be designed for here rather than discovered in the wallet: **signing two different messages with one WOTS+ key reveals the private key.** Not degraded security — anyone holding both signatures can spend everything that key owns. Keys are therefore per-note, and a wallet must never sign twice.

`formal/onetime.qnt` checks what that discipline costs, and the answer is that the obvious version of it is safe and **freezes live money**:

| Discipline | Key ever signs twice? | Note always spendable? |
|---|---|---|
| Trust the wallet's own memory | **no — total loss** | no |
| Persist "this key has signed" | yes, safe | **no — permanent freeze** |
| Persist *what* was signed, and replay it | yes, safe | yes |

The freeze is not exotic and needs no backup restore. Sign a transfer; its record loses a first-occurrence race or a reorg drops it. Bitcoin still shows the coin unspent, so the wallet sees it as spendable — and the log says the key has signed. A wallet that only knows *that* it signed can do nothing but refuse, forever, while the coin sits there.

**The rule, therefore: the sign log records the payload, not a flag.** WOTS+ signing is deterministic — one key and one message produce one signature, byte for byte — so re-signing the *identical* payload reproduces the signature that already exists and discloses nothing new. Rebroadcast replays; it never re-signs something different. This is what makes the third row of that table possible, and it is the reason the log must be a record of transfers rather than a set of spent-key markers.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. Determinism is now **load-bearing**, so a hedged or blinded signing variant would silently convert this recovery path into key disclosure; it is pinned by `air/src/wots.rs::signing_is_deterministic`. And a wallet does *not* need persisted state to learn which notes settled: nullifiers are derived deterministically from the note, so rescanning the chain recovers that for free. Persistence is load-bearing only for the window between signing and settlement.

## Recovery

One seed derives scan and spend keys; seed + the network's storage layer reconstructs every note and proof. Wallet backup is "write down the seed" — and with one-time spend keys that is no longer the *whole* story. The seed recovers every key and, by rescanning for derived nullifiers, every settled note. What it cannot recover is the payload of a transfer that was signed but never settled, and by the section above that payload is what makes such a note spendable again. **So the sign log is part of the backup, not a cache.** Lose it and you lose only in-flight transfers — bounded, and far short of the total loss that re-signing blindly would cause — but "write down the seed" alone no longer means losing a device can never lose funds.

## What self-custody does and doesn't mean (audit #13 E2)

Holding a note means holding **the bearer instrument** — nobody can freeze, seize, or spend it without your key, and no custodian sits between you and it. That is the whole point and it is true.

It does **not** mean you are free of the issuer. For any asset that represents something off-chain — a dollar stablecoin, a tokenized share — the issuer still controls the reserve, the redemption window, and whether they will honor *your* note. An issuer can refuse redemption, blacklist an identity at the redemption boundary, or go insolvent, and no amount of cryptography here changes that. Ultraviolet removes the custodian from **holding and transferring**; it cannot remove the issuer from **backing and redeeming**.

Native assets (a collectible, a governance token with no off-chain claim) have no such dependency — there is nothing to redeem. Say which kind you're describing.
